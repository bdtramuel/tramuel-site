There are certain things that happen in the course of an ordinary week that would be easy to dismiss as insignificant.

A pair of shoes arrives.

A group of friends finally manages to make a brunch happen.

You find yourself looking at watches and realizing that, apparently, everyone has decided that a man's wrist needs to look like a dinner plate.

And then you buy an old Schwinn for a hundred dollars because you liked the way it looked.

None of these things are important.

Which is probably why I like them.

**The shoes**

I ordered a pair of Vibae shoes from Portugal on a Thursday.

They arrived Tuesday.

Technically, they probably arrived Monday, but Monday was Labor Day, so Tuesday is the day they officially entered my life.

Either way: Portugal to my front door in a few days.

I was impressed.

Maybe *too* impressed.

Because there is something dangerous about discovering that you can order something expensive from another continent on Thursday and have it sitting in your house by Tuesday.

I want instant gratification.

I know this about myself.

If I'm going to spend real money on something, I don't necessarily want to spend the next two weeks wondering where it is. I want the little dopamine hit of the purchase to be followed immediately by the physical evidence of the purchase.

There it is.

The box.

The shoes.

Mine.

Of course, the journey was its own little story. Somewhere between Portugal and me, a series of people and machines moved those shoes across an ocean and through a network of places I will never see.

They had a life before they became mine.

And then, suddenly, they were on my doorstep.

I opened the box.

Worth the wait.

Which, at four or five days, barely qualifies as a wait anymore.

We've come a long way from ordering something and being told to allow 6–8 weeks for delivery.

I'm not sure that's progress.

But I like it.

**Five brothers and a sister**

There are six of us.

Five brothers and one sister.

Not actually related.

But that distinction has become less important over time.

We normally get together once a month.

Or at least that is what we tell ourselves.

Then life happens.

Work.

Family.

Travel.

Responsibilities.

People get tired.

Calendars get stupid.

And somehow a month becomes two months, then three, and eventually you realize that the people you routinely see aren't people you've seen in a long while.

So when the text finally came:

***Brunch?***

That was enough.

We picked a day.

Picked a time.

Picked a general area.

And stopped.

No 47-message group-text referendum.

No spreadsheet.

No elaborate coordination exercise.

No need to decide everything in advance.

We'll figure it out.

Someone will suggest a place.

Someone else will veto it.

Someone will be late.

Someone will say they don't care where we eat and then care very deeply where we eat.

Someone will probably say, "Yooo, totally forgot about Rob..."

And we'll laugh.

Because that's what this is.

Not an event.

Not a reunion.

Just six people who needed to see each other again.

Sometimes the logistics don't need to be perfect.

Sometimes you just need a day, a time and a general location.

Everything else can fall into place.

We need this.

Five brothers and one sister.

Not actually related.

But close enough.

**The watch**

I've also been looking for a birth-year watch.

That search has turned into something slightly more interesting than shopping for a watch.

I'm noticing what I don't want.

The trend right now seems to favor big watches.

Big faces.

Big wrists.

Big presence.

Forty-four millimeters.

Forty-six.

Maybe bigger.

Everything announcing itself.

And I keep coming back to something much smaller.

Thirty-four to thirty-eight millimeters.

***The word underneath all of it is deliberate.***

I'm not looking for the biggest watch in the room. I'm not particularly interested in whatever happens to be having a moment.

I'm looking for the thing that makes sense to me.

Something I could have bought then, worn now, and still understand.

Something with enough presence to be noticed without asking to be noticed.

I'm looking for something from my birth year, which already gives the search a little meaning.

But I don't want something that feels like a museum piece.

I want something that feels like it could have belonged to me all along.

Something restrained.

Something intentional.

Mine.

Maybe that's what I'm learning to appreciate more these days: the difference between choosing something because it's what everyone is choosing and choosing something because you've actually decided who you are.

There is always something new to chase.

The current silhouette.

The current color.

The current size.

The thing everybody suddenly knows about.

But eventually you have to decide whether you actually like the thing or whether you like being able to recognize the thing as *current*.

I've got the Vague for the sportier side of things.

What I'm looking for now is its opposite.

Something that doesn't need to explain itself.

Something that will look right five years from now, twenty years from now, maybe forty.

The kind of thing that gets better at being itself with age.

Which, if I'm honest, is probably what I'm looking for in a lot of things these days.

**The Schwinn**

And then I bought a bicycle.

Not a new bicycle.

Not some carbon-fiber, wind-tunnel-tested, electronically shifted machine with a name that sounds like a fighter jet.

A vintage Schwinn.

Blue steel.

A classic frame.

A Brooks saddle in that beautiful saddle brown.

Brooks brown handlebar tape to match.

A hundred dollars.

I wasn't even sure I was going to buy it.

I was trying to save myself a trip, actually.

The seller said he was six feet tall and that it fit him. The pictures looked promising. The proportions looked like they might work for me.

But pictures lie.

So I went to sit on it.

And apparently that was all it took.

Sometimes you don't need to know exactly what something is going to become before you decide to take it home.

You just need to recognize the possibility.

The Brooks saddle alone made the hundred dollars feel reasonable.

Then he handed me a box containing a fresh roll of Brooks tape.

Apparently he'd wrapped the bars himself.

It showed.

Which I say with affection.

The tape wasn't terrible. It just wasn't *finished*.

So tomorrow it'll go to the shop for a tune-up, a proper rewrap, and a once-over of everything that has been sitting around for years waiting for somebody to care about it again.

I'll probably raise the saddle.

Raise the handlebars.

I like a higher ride.

Not because the bike needs to become something it isn't.

Because it needs to become something **I can live in**.

That's the distinction.

I'm not trying to restore it to some perfect historical moment.

I'm not particularly interested in whether every component remains exactly as Schwinn intended.

I want the steel frame.

I want the brown leather.

I want the patina.

I want it to look like it belongs to somebody who has been riding it for years, even if I just got it.

A classic thing doesn't have to remain untouched to remain classic.

Sometimes you make a few changes.

Sometimes you replace what's worn.

Sometimes you wrap the handlebars again.

Sometimes you raise the seat.

And then you ride it.

There is something satisfying about that.

Maybe because I'm increasingly drawn to things that don't need to be new to be valuable.

Things with some history.

Things with some evidence of having been lived with.

Things that can be adjusted without being discarded.

The shoes were new.

The watch I'm looking for will be old.

The bike already is.

And somehow they all feel like part of the same conversation.

**Deliberate**

Maybe that's the thing underneath all of this.

Not the shoes.

Not brunch.

Not the watch.

Not even the bicycle.

**Deliberate.**

I'm becoming more interested in choosing than accumulating.

In knowing why something belongs with me.

In allowing some things to be exactly what they are and changing other things until they fit.

The shoes were chosen because I wanted them.

The brunch was chosen because we needed each other.

The watch is being chosen because I know what feels like me.

And the Schwinn?

The Schwinn was chosen because I saw something worth bringing home.

Maybe that's enough.

Life doesn't always announce itself as a lesson.

Sometimes it's just a package from Portugal, a brunch text, a 36-millimeter watch you're still looking for, and a hundred-dollar Schwinn with a Brooks saddle.

Small things.

Nothing particularly profound.

But maybe that's the point.

I'm not sure I'm looking for Big and Important anymore.
